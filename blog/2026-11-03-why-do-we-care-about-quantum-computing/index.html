Why do we care about Quantum Computing?

November 3, 2026
quantum-computingcryptographymathematicscomputer-security

A sufficiently capable quantum computer could recover the private keys behind much of the public-key cryptography we use. It could read communications protected by vulnerable key exchanges and forge signatures that we rely on to authenticate people, computers, and software updates.

The machine needed to do this is called a cryptographically relevant quantum computer, or CRQC. It must be able to complete a cryptographic attack at a useful key size and within a useful amount of time. Its requirements include enough logical qubits, sufficiently reliable operations, and an enormous amount of classical computation to keep the quantum computation working.

A physical qubit is a physical system in which we use two distinguishable quantum states to represent ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ and ∣1⟩|1\rangle∣1⟩. They might be two internal states of an atom or ion, or two energy levels of a superconducting circuit. We must be able to prepare the system, manipulate it, couple it to other qubits, and measure it.

A logical qubit is encoded across multiple physical qubits using an error-correcting code.

The notation ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ and ∣1⟩|1\rangle∣1⟩, pronounced “ket zero” and “ket one,” denotes two basis vectors, just as (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) and (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) do in linear algebra. A superposition is a normalized linear combination of them:

∣ψ⟩=α∣0⟩+β∣1⟩.|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle.∣ψ⟩=α∣0⟩+β∣1⟩.

The coefficients α\alphaα and β\betaβ are called amplitudes. Measuring in this basis gives zero with probability ∣α∣2|\alpha|^2∣α∣2 and one with probability ∣β∣2|\beta|^2∣β∣2, with ∣α∣2+∣β∣2=1|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1∣α∣2+∣β∣2=1. Writing a complex amplitude as ρeiγ\rho e^{i\gamma}ρeiγ, ρ\rhoρ is its magnitude and γ\gammaγ is its phase, the angle in the complex plane. The relative phase is the difference between the two amplitudes' angles.

When an operation combines contributions to the same measurement outcome, their amplitudes add before we take the squared magnitude. Contributions with matching phases reinforce one another; opposing contributions can cancel. This is interference.

The usual geometric representation is the Bloch sphere. Ignoring a common phase multiplying the entire vector, which does not change measurement probabilities, we can write a pure single-qubit state as

∣ψ⟩=cos⁡(θ/2)∣0⟩+eiϕsin⁡(θ/2)∣1⟩.|\psi\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle +e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle.∣ψ⟩=cos(θ/2)∣0⟩+eiϕsin(θ/2)∣1⟩.

The polar angle θ\thetaθ sets the measurement probabilities: cos⁡2(θ/2)\cos^2(\theta/2)cos2(θ/2) for zero and sin⁡2(θ/2)\sin^2(\theta/2)sin2(θ/2) for one. The angle ϕ\phiϕ is the relative phase between the two amplitudes. Every point on the equator gives equal zero/one probabilities, although those states behave differently in subsequent operations.

A point on the surface represents a pure state, one described by a single state vector. Preparing ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ or ∣1⟩|1\rangle∣1⟩ with equal classical probabilities gives a statistical mixture at the center; preparing (∣0⟩+∣1⟩)/2(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2(∣0⟩+∣1⟩)/2​ gives a pure state on the equator. A reversible single-qubit gate moves the point by rotating the sphere's radius vector. This is what “rotation” means here: a controlled change of the quantum state. A relative phase shift moves the point around the vertical axis while preserving its zero/one measurement probabilities.

An ordinary digital circuit represents zero and one by ranges of voltages. Small disturbances within those ranges can be removed as gates regenerate the signal. A qubit must preserve the amplitudes and relative phase needed by the computation. An unwanted phase change can spoil the answer even when the probabilities of immediately measuring zero or one have not changed.

Physical qubits interact with their surroundings. An excited (higher-energy) state can lose energy to the environment and decay. Fluctuating electric or magnetic fields can change the relative phase. Uncontrolled interaction with the environment can carry away information about the qubit and destroy coherence, the phase relationships needed for interference. These processes are called relaxation and dephasing.

Control introduces further opportunities for error. A microwave or laser pulse can change the amplitudes or relative phase by the wrong amount. A signal intended for one qubit can disturb its neighbor. The engineering problem is to suppress unwanted interactions while retaining the deliberate interactions needed to compute. Cooling, shielding, vacuum systems, and precisely controlled fields address different parts of that problem, depending on the hardware.

The encoding of a logical qubit distributes its information so that certain errors can be detected and corrected without learning the encoded amplitudes. It is not a collection of identical backups: the no-cloning theorem rules out copying an arbitrary unknown quantum state.

The code repeatedly measures selected relationships among physical qubits. Those measurements produce error syndromes, which a classical decoder interprets. The checks reveal evidence of errors while preserving the logical information. More physical qubits can provide stronger protection when the hardware and code meet the necessary conditions. There is no fixed conversion rate from physical qubits to logical qubits.

RSA starts with two large primes, ppp and qqq, and publishes their product:

N=pq.N=pq.N=pq.

Multiplying the primes is easy. Recovering them from a properly chosen, sufficiently large NNN is beyond the reach of the best known classical factoring methods. Someone who factors NNN can calculate the private exponent from the public exponent. That compromises both RSA encryption and RSA signatures.

Diffie–Hellman key exchange uses a different problem. In a suitable group, Alice publishes gag^aga and Bob publishes gbg^bgb. Their exponents remain secret. Each can calculate gabg^{ab}gab using the other person's public value and their own exponent. An eavesdropper sees the public values, but recovering an exponent requires solving a discrete logarithm problem. An efficient discrete-logarithm algorithm therefore breaks the exchange.

Elliptic-curve cryptography changes the group. Its public key is a point P=aGP=aGP=aG, obtained by adding a publicly known point GGG to itself aaa times. The secret is aaa. Classical attacks on well-chosen curves are expensive, which allows much shorter keys than RSA at comparable classical security levels. But Shor's discrete-logarithm algorithm applies to elliptic curves too. ECDH key exchange and elliptic-curve signatures, including ECDSA and EdDSA, are vulnerable.

These attacks exploit periods and discrete logarithms in efficiently computable abelian groups, where combining elements in either order gives the same result. Shor solves the problems on which RSA and these discrete-logarithm systems depend. Merely using a group does not mean a cryptosystem's security depends on either problem.

Peter Shor's result makes factoring and discrete logarithms possible in polynomial time on a suitable quantum computer. Here, polynomial time means that the number of operations grows as a polynomial in the number of bits used to describe the problem. Increasing an RSA key's length makes the quantum attack more expensive, but it does not restore the mathematical obstacle that protects it against known classical attacks.

To reduce factoring to period finding, choose an integer aaa that is relatively prime to NNN. We can check that condition efficiently with Euclid's algorithm. Then consider

f(x)=ax mod N.f(x)=a^x\bmod N.f(x)=axmodN.

The values repeat. The smallest positive integer rrr for which ar≡1(modN)a^r\equiv1\pmod Nar≡1(modN) is the order of aaa modulo NNN, and it is the period of fff.

Suppose we can find rrr. If it is even, let z=ar/2 mod Nz=a^{r/2}\bmod Nz=ar/2modN. Then

z2≡1(modN),(z−1)(z+1)≡0(modN).z^2\equiv1\pmod N, \qquad (z-1)(z+1)\equiv0\pmod N.z2≡1(modN),(z−1)(z+1)≡0(modN).

When zzz is neither 111 nor −1-1−1 modulo NNN, calculating gcd⁡(z−1,N)\gcd(z-1,N)gcd(z−1,N) and gcd⁡(z+1,N)\gcd(z+1,N)gcd(z+1,N) yields nontrivial factors. If the order is odd or the result is unhelpful, we try another choice of aaa. The greatest-common-divisor calculations are ordinary classical arithmetic. The difficult part is finding the period.

For a small example, take N=21N=21N=21 and a=2a=2a=2. The successive powers, reduced modulo 212121, are

1,  2,  4,  8,  16,  11,  1,  2,…1,\;2,\;4,\;8,\;16,\;11,\;1,\;2,\ldots1,2,4,8,16,11,1,2,…

gcd⁡(8−1,21)=7,gcd⁡(8+1,21)=3.\gcd(8-1,21)=7, \qquad \gcd(8+1,21)=3.gcd(8−1,21)=7,gcd(8+1,21)=3.

We have factored 212121 by finding a period. The same reduction works for a large RSA modulus. Enumerating its powers until they repeat would take too long, so we need another way to discover the period.

A register is a collection of qubits treated as one system. An mmm-qubit register has 2m2^m2m computational-basis states, labeled by the possible bit strings or their integer values. Its state vector has one complex amplitude per basis state. This is a mathematical description of the state; measuring the register returns one bit string.

A quantum algorithm arranges interference among these amplitudes so that a useful measurement becomes likely.

For period finding, prepare a uniform superposition of integers from 000 through Q−1Q-1Q−1, where QQQ is a power of two: each has the same amplitude, initially with the same phase. Reversible modular arithmetic then computes ax mod Na^x\bmod NaxmodN into a second register:

1Q∑x=0Q−1∣x⟩∣ax mod N⟩.\frac{1}{\sqrt Q} \sum_{x=0}^{Q-1}|x\rangle|a^x\bmod N\rangle.Q​1​x=0∑Q−1​∣x⟩∣axmodN⟩.

Two kets written together specify the values in the two registers.

The two registers are entangled: their joint state cannot be written as an independent state for each register. The second register's value is linked to the first register's input. For the explanation, imagine measuring the second register and obtaining one particular function value. The first register is then a superposition of the inputs that produced that value:

∣ψ⟩=1L∑j=0L−1∣x0+jr⟩.|\psi\rangle= \frac{1}{\sqrt L} \sum_{j=0}^{L-1}|x_0+jr\rangle.∣ψ⟩=L​1​j=0∑L−1​∣x0​+jr⟩.

Here LLL counts the terms that fit inside the register, and x0x_0x0​ is the smallest nonnegative input giving the measured function value. The terms are evenly spaced by the unknown period rrr. Measuring this register immediately would give us one of those inputs. Instead, we change from the basis labeled by input values to a basis that reveals frequencies.

The quantum Fourier transform acts on each basis state as follows:

FQ∣x⟩=1Q∑k=0Q−1e2πixk/Q∣k⟩.F_Q|x\rangle= \frac{1}{\sqrt Q} \sum_{k=0}^{Q-1}e^{2\pi i xk/Q}|k\rangle.FQ​∣x⟩=Q​1​k=0∑Q−1​e2πixk/Q∣k⟩.

For our periodic state, the amplitude of a possible output kkk contains the sum

∑j=0L−1e2πijrk/Q.\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}e^{2\pi i jrk/Q}.j=0∑L−1​e2πijrk/Q.

When rk/Qrk/Qrk/Q is an integer, the terms have the same phase and reinforce one another. At other values, the phases rotate around the circle and tend to cancel. The measurement probabilities concentrate near

kQ=sr\frac{k}{Q}=\frac{s}{r}Qk​=rs​

for integer values of sss. The unknown spacing in the input has become a pattern of likely frequencies in the output.

If rrr divides QQQ, the peaks are exact. Otherwise, they fall near the corresponding frequencies. Choosing QQQ large enough, conventionally at least N2N^2N2 in this version of the algorithm, gives enough precision for continued fractions, a classical method for finding accurate rational approximations, to recover candidate periods.

For Q=2mQ=2^mQ=2m, the transform's standard circuit uses only O(m2)O(m^2)O(m2) elementary gates. These operations mix amplitudes and make conditional phase shifts, changing a component's complex angle depending on another qubit's value. It transforms 2m2^m2m amplitudes without requiring 2m2^m2m separate gates. Implementing those operations accurately on error-corrected hardware adds work. We do not get to read out all those amplitudes. A measurement gives one kkk. The carefully prepared periodic state is what makes a few such measurements useful.

The discrete-logarithm version uses related interference in two dimensions. Let ggg generate a cyclic group of prime order ℓ\ellℓ, so its powers run through all ℓ\ellℓ elements. Suppose y=gsy=g^sy=gs, and sss is secret. Take uuu and vvv modulo ℓ\ellℓ and consider

h(u,v)=guyv=gu+sv.h(u,v)=g^u y^v=g^{u+sv}.h(u,v)=guyv=gu+sv.

Changing (u,v)(u,v)(u,v) by (−s,1)(-s,1)(−s,1) leaves the value unchanged. Prepare a uniform superposition of the pairs (u,v)(u,v)(u,v) and compute h(u,v)h(u,v)h(u,v) into a third register. Apply a Fourier transform modulo ℓ\ellℓ to each input register. Measuring those registers gives two frequencies, ccc and ddd, related by

d≡cs(modℓ).d\equiv cs\pmod\ell.d≡cs(modℓ).

For a nonzero ccc, ordinary modular inversion gives sss. With elliptic curves, we write the function as uG+vPuG+vPuG+vP instead. The same hidden relation is present. Changing from multiplication modulo a prime to addition of elliptic-curve points does not remove it.

The logical qubits must support reliable gates as well as reliable storage. The machine must perform a long sequence of reversible arithmetic operations, preserve the information throughout, and read out the result. For factoring, modular exponentiation accounts for much of the work. The Fourier transform is a comparatively small part of the circuit.

A classical circuit can be built entirely from NAND gates, or entirely from NOR gates. Each is universal for Boolean logic: it can express every Boolean function, given enough gates and wires. For NAND, tying the two inputs together produces NOT. Negating a NAND output produces AND. Applying NAND to two inverted inputs produces OR, by De Morgan's law.

The small circle at a gate's output means inversion. NAND alone can build NOT, AND, and OR; NOR has the same universality property. Practical circuit designs use a broader gate library to improve area, power, and delay.

A quantum gate acts on the state vector. For the computation between measurements, it is a unitary transformation: a reversible linear map that preserves the sum of the squared magnitudes of the amplitudes, and therefore total probability. An ordinary AND gate maps three different inputs to zero, so its output cannot tell us which input was supplied. We can implement that Boolean function reversibly by preserving its inputs and writing the result into an additional qubit.

That is one job for a Toffoli gate, also called controlled-controlled-NOT. It has two control qubits, aaa and bbb, and a target qubit, ccc. On computational-basis inputs, it leaves aaa and bbb unchanged and flips ccc exactly when both controls are one:

∣a,b,c⟩⟼∣a,b,c⊕(a∧b)⟩.|a,b,c\rangle\longmapsto |a,b,c\oplus(a\land b)\rangle.∣a,b,c⟩⟼∣a,b,c⊕(a∧b)⟩.

Here ⊕\oplus⊕ is exclusive-or and ∧\land∧ is AND. Start the target at zero and it receives a∧ba\land ba∧b. Apply the gate twice and the original state is restored. Together with simpler reversible gates, Toffoli gates build adders, multipliers, and other Boolean computations. In a quantum circuit, the operation acts on superpositions without measuring the controls. That is how we can perform the modular arithmetic in Shor's algorithm coherently.

Reversible Boolean gates alone just permute computational-basis states. Quantum universality also requires control over superpositions and phases. The Hadamard gate, HHH, mixes the zero and one amplitudes. The T gate advances the phase of the one component relative to the zero component by π/4\pi/4π/4. CNOT provides an interaction that can entangle two qubits.

The set {H,T,CNOT}\{H,T,\mathrm{CNOT}\}{H,T,CNOT} is universal for quantum computation: circuits made from these gates can approximate any unitary operation to any desired accuracy, up to an unobservable overall phase. More precision generally requires more gates.

The Clifford gates are the operations that can be built from HHH, CNOT, and the phase gate SSS, which advances the relative phase by π/2\pi/2π/2. Starting with qubits initialized to ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩, Clifford gates and measurements in the zero/one basis alone give circuits that can be simulated efficiently on a classical computer. They do not provide universal quantum computation.

The T and Toffoli gates are non-Clifford operations: they lie outside that restricted family. A common error-correcting scheme, the surface code, uses local checks on a two-dimensional arrangement of qubits. Computation must be fault-tolerant, meaning that errors in individual components, including the correction machinery, are contained rather than allowed to spread into an uncorrectable logical error.

In surface-code architectures, supplying non-Clifford operations requires additional machinery. One approach consumes specially prepared auxiliary states called magic states. For example, the state used to supply a T gate is

∣A⟩=∣0⟩+eiπ/4∣1⟩2.|A\rangle= \frac{|0\rangle+e^{i\pi/4}|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.∣A⟩=2​∣0⟩+eiπ/4∣1⟩​.

The magic-state construction combines these resources with Clifford gates, measurements, and classical feedback to perform the required operation on the data. This is called gate injection: consuming an auxiliary state to implement a gate. Feed-forward means using a measurement result to choose a subsequent operation. Preparing the resource state supplies what the Clifford operations cannot do alone.

A factory supplies auxiliary quantum states to the computation. Clifford operations, measurements, and classical feedback use them to enact non-Clifford gates. Encoded states throughout this system still require error correction.

A magic-state factory dedicates physical qubits, gates, and time to producing those states reliably. Distillation protocols consume multiple imperfect resource states to obtain fewer, cleaner ones. Other approaches, including magic-state cultivation, change how the states are prepared and checked. The supply rate matters: a computation that needs these states cannot simply run ahead when the factory has not produced them.

In March 2026, Google's researchers reported two circuit designs for a 256-bit elliptic-curve discrete-logarithm problem:

These are logical resource estimates. Each protected Toffoli operation can require many physical operations and additional auxiliary states. Turning a gate count into a runtime requires the circuit's dependencies, opportunities for parallelism, factory throughput, and classical response times.

Under its superconducting-hardware assumptions, Google's study estimated fewer than 500,000 physical qubits and a runtime of minutes for this elliptic-curve attack.

For RSA-2048, Craig Gidney's 2025 estimate put the physical requirement below a million noisy qubits, with a runtime below a week, under specified assumptions about gate errors, connectivity, error-correction cycles, and classical control. These numbers change as algorithms, codes, and hardware improve. There is no single qubit count that defines a CRQC independently of its target and operating conditions.

Error correction remains a substantial part of the machine. An uncorrected physical error probability that looks small for one gate is disastrous over a long computation. As a simple illustration, a billion vulnerable operations, each independently failing with probability 10−610^{-6}10−6, would produce about a thousand failures on average. We need a much smaller logical error rate across the whole computation.

An error-correction scheme has an error threshold: under its assumed noise model, this is the physical error level below which adding redundancy improves protection. Surface-code constructions show how to do this, including the additional machinery needed for a universal set of logical gates.

The syndrome measurements go to a classical decoder. It must infer the likely errors and determine the corrections, often tracking them in a classical record rather than immediately applying a physical correction. That computation continues throughout the quantum calculation.

Both the bandwidth and the computation can be enormous. If 100,000 error checks each produce one syndrome bit every microsecond, the raw stream is 101110^{11}1011 bits per second: 12.5 gigabytes per second. An illustrative larger system with ten million such checks would produce 1.25 terabytes per second, before compression or communications overhead. The measurements continue throughout the calculation, potentially for hours or days.

The decoder must compare check results from neighboring qubits over successive rounds to infer likely error patterns. Under their surface-code and decoder assumptions, Camps and colleagues estimated petaflop-scale classical capacity for decoding in a 1,000-logical-qubit chemistry calculation: roughly 101510^{15}1015 floating-point operations per second. Their model converts CPU decoder timings into equivalent floating-point capacity.

Throughput and latency both matter. The classical system must keep pace with incoming measurements, and it must deliver the information needed by later quantum operations. A growing backlog slows the computation. Google's Willow real-time decoding experiment ran error-correction rounds every 1.11.11.1 microseconds, while decoder latency averaged 636363 microseconds.

Reducing the logical-qubit requirement does not eliminate the physical-qubit overhead, the gate count, or the classical decoding workload. A CRQC is an integrated quantum and classical computer.

Conflating artificial intelligence with quantum computing is nonsense. Today's mainstream AI systems are trained and run on classical computers. Shor's algorithm does not depend on a trained model. There are specific research connections, including neural networks for decoding quantum error syndromes. In that work, classical software interprets the quantum processor's error measurements. It does not make the two technologies interchangeable. A sales pitch that simply bundles “AI” and “quantum” together is asking for your money with two buzzwords.

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) supplies replacements for vulnerable key exchanges and signature schemes, using different mathematical assumptions. It runs on classical computers. NIST's first three finalized standards cover key establishment and digital signatures using lattice-based and hash-based constructions. Shor's algorithm does not solve their underlying problems.

For readers interested in the algorithms, I have implemented classical and post-quantum cryptography in pure Rust. The code is available in my cryptography repository.

The timing depends partly on how long information must remain secret. An adversary can retain a vulnerable key exchange and the encrypted traffic associated with it, then attack the exchange when a suitable machine becomes available. NIST calls this harvest now, decrypt later. Upgrading the systems later does not retrieve the copies already recorded.
