Why do we care about Quantum Computing?

November 3, 2026
quantum-computingcryptographymathematicscomputer-security

Public-key cryptography lets computers establish shared secrets over untrusted networks and verify digital signatures. We use it when a browser authenticates a website, an SSH client authenticates a server, or an operating system checks a software update's signature.

A sufficiently capable quantum computer could recover the private keys behind much of the public-key cryptography we use. It could read communications protected by vulnerable key exchanges and forge signatures that we rely on to authenticate people, computers, and software updates.

The machine needed to do this is called a cryptographically relevant quantum computer, or CRQC. It must be able to complete a cryptographic attack at a useful key size and within a useful amount of time.

One target is RSA, a public-key cryptosystem for encryption and digital signatures, named for Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman. For encryption, a sender uses the recipient's public key, and the recipient decrypts with the private key. For signatures, the owner signs with the private key, and others verify the signature with the public key.

RSA constructs its key pair using two large, distinct primes, ppp and qqq. Their product, called the modulus, is part of the public key:

N=pq.N=pq.N=pq.

Multiplying the primes is easy. Recovering them from a properly chosen, sufficiently large NNN is beyond the reach of the best known classical factoring methods. Someone who factors NNN can reconstruct the private key from the public key. That compromises both RSA encryption and RSA signatures.

Diffie–Hellman key exchange lets two parties establish a shared secret over a public network. They can use that secret to derive keys for encrypting their traffic. It relies on a different mathematical problem from factoring. In the modular-arithmetic version, they agree on a suitable prime ppp and a public base ggg. Alice publishes ga mod pg^a\bmod pgamodp and Bob publishes gb mod pg^b\bmod pgbmodp. Their exponents remain secret. Each can calculate gab mod pg^{ab}\bmod pgabmodp using the other person's public value and their own exponent. An eavesdropper sees the public values, but recovering an exponent requires solving a discrete logarithm problem. Recovering either exponent lets an eavesdropper calculate the shared secret.

Elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC) is a family of public-key schemes for key exchange and digital signatures. Its arithmetic adds points on an elliptic curve. A public key is a point P=aGP=aGP=aG, obtained by adding a publicly known point GGG to itself aaa times. The secret is aaa. Classical attacks on well-chosen curves are expensive, which allows much shorter keys than RSA at comparable classical security levels. But quantum discrete-logarithm algorithms apply to elliptic curves too, making their key exchanges and digital signatures vulnerable.

A mathematical group is a set with an associative combining operation, an identity element, and an inverse for each element. In an abelian group, combining two elements in either order gives the same result. These quantum attacks exploit periods and discrete logarithms in such groups when their operations can be computed efficiently. Factoring and discrete logarithms are the problems on which these cryptosystems depend. Merely using a group does not mean a cryptosystem's security depends on either problem.

Peter Shor's result makes factoring and discrete logarithms possible in polynomial time on a suitable quantum computer. Polynomial time means that the number of operations grows as a polynomial in the number of bits used to describe the problem. Increasing an RSA key's length makes the quantum attack more expensive, but it does not restore the mathematical obstacle that protects it against known classical attacks.

A physical qubit is a physical system in which we use two distinguishable quantum states to represent ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ and ∣1⟩|1\rangle∣1⟩. They might be two internal states of an atom or ion, or two energy levels of a superconducting circuit. We must be able to prepare the system, manipulate it, couple it to other qubits, and measure it.

A logical qubit is encoded across multiple physical qubits using a quantum error-correcting code: a mathematical scheme that adds redundancy so errors can be detected and corrected. A CRQC needs enough logical qubits, reliable gates, and an enormous amount of classical computation to keep the quantum computation working.

The notation ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ and ∣1⟩|1\rangle∣1⟩, pronounced “ket zero” and “ket one,” denotes two basis vectors, just as (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) and (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) do in linear algebra. A superposition is a normalized linear combination of them:

∣ψ⟩=α∣0⟩+β∣1⟩.|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle.∣ψ⟩=α∣0⟩+β∣1⟩.

The coefficients α\alphaα and β\betaβ are called amplitudes. The pair (α,β)(\alpha,\beta)(α,β) is the state vector in this basis. Measuring in this basis gives zero with probability ∣α∣2|\alpha|^2∣α∣2 and one with probability ∣β∣2|\beta|^2∣β∣2, with ∣α∣2+∣β∣2=1|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1∣α∣2+∣β∣2=1.

A complex amplitude ρeiγ\rho e^{i\gamma}ρeiγ has magnitude ρ\rhoρ and phase γ\gammaγ, its angle in the complex plane. Euler's formula gives eiγ=cos⁡γ+isin⁡γe^{i\gamma}=\cos\gamma+i\sin\gammaeiγ=cosγ+isinγ, where i2=−1i^2=-1i2=−1. The relative phase is the difference between the two amplitudes' angles.

When an operation combines contributions to the same measurement outcome, their amplitudes add before we take the squared magnitude. Contributions with matching phases reinforce one another; opposing contributions can cancel. This is interference.

A quantum gate acts on the state vector. For the computation between measurements, it is a unitary transformation: a reversible linear map that preserves the sum of the squared magnitudes of the amplitudes, keeping total probability equal to one.

A pure state is one described by a single state vector. The usual geometric representation is the Bloch sphere. Ignoring a common phase multiplying the entire vector, which does not change measurement probabilities, we can write a pure single-qubit state as

∣ψ⟩=cos⁡(θ/2)∣0⟩+eiϕsin⁡(θ/2)∣1⟩.|\psi\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle +e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle.∣ψ⟩=cos(θ/2)∣0⟩+eiϕsin(θ/2)∣1⟩.

The polar angle θ\thetaθ sets the measurement probabilities: cos⁡2(θ/2)\cos^2(\theta/2)cos2(θ/2) for zero and sin⁡2(θ/2)\sin^2(\theta/2)sin2(θ/2) for one. The angle ϕ\phiϕ is the relative phase between the two amplitudes. Every point on the equator gives equal zero/one probabilities, although those states behave differently in subsequent operations.

A pure state corresponds to a point on the sphere's surface. Preparing ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ or ∣1⟩|1\rangle∣1⟩ with equal classical probabilities gives a statistical mixture at the center; preparing (∣0⟩+∣1⟩)/2(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2(∣0⟩+∣1⟩)/2​ gives a pure state on the equator. A reversible single-qubit gate moves the point by rotating the sphere's radius vector. This is what “rotation” means here: a controlled change of the quantum state. A relative phase shift moves the point around the vertical axis while preserving its zero/one measurement probabilities.

A classical circuit can be built entirely from NAND gates, or entirely from NOR gates. Each is universal for Boolean logic: it can express every Boolean function, given enough gates and wires. For NAND, tying the two inputs together produces NOT. Negating a NAND output produces AND. Applying NAND to two inverted inputs produces OR, by De Morgan's law.

The small circle at a gate's output means inversion. NAND alone can build NOT, AND, and OR; NOR has the same universality property. Practical circuit designs use a broader gate library to improve area, power, and delay.

For nnn qubits, the state vector has 2n2^n2n amplitudes, one for each possible nnn-bit string. Two qubits have four amplitudes; three have eight. The notation ∣a,b,c⟩|a,b,c\rangle∣a,b,c⟩ labels the basis state for the bit string abcabcabc: its amplitude is one and all other entries are zero.

To implement Boolean logic with quantum gates, the computation must be reversible. An AND gate maps three different inputs to zero, so its output cannot tell us which input was supplied. We can implement that Boolean function reversibly by preserving its inputs and writing the result into an additional qubit.

That is one job for a Toffoli gate, also called controlled-controlled-NOT. It has two control qubits, aaa and bbb, and a target qubit, ccc. For inputs with definite bit values, it leaves aaa and bbb unchanged and flips ccc exactly when both controls are one:

(a,b,c)⟼(a,b,c⊕(a∧b)).(a,b,c)\longmapsto (a,b,c\oplus(a\land b)).(a,b,c)⟼(a,b,c⊕(a∧b)).

⊕\oplus⊕ is exclusive-or and ∧\land∧ is AND. Start the target at zero and it receives a∧ba\land ba∧b. Apply the gate twice and the original state is restored. Together with simpler reversible gates, Toffoli gates build adders, multipliers, and other Boolean computations. In a quantum circuit, the operation acts on superpositions without measuring the controls. A reversible arithmetic circuit acts on a superposition of inputs while preserving their phase relationships.

Reversible Boolean gates alone permute the possible bit strings. Quantum universality also requires control over superpositions and phases. The Hadamard gate, HHH, replaces a qubit's zero and one amplitudes (u,v)(u,v)(u,v) with ((u+v)/2,(u−v)/2)((u+v)/\sqrt2,(u-v)/\sqrt2)((u+v)/2​,(u−v)/2​). The T gate advances the phase of the one component relative to the zero component by π/4\pi/4π/4. CNOT, or controlled-NOT, flips a target qubit when a single control qubit is one, leaving the control unchanged.

The Hadamard rule shows why phase matters. The amplitude pairs (1,1)/2(1,1)/\sqrt2(1,1)/2​ and (1,−1)/2(1,-1)/\sqrt2(1,−1)/2​ both give equal zero/one probabilities when measured. After a Hadamard, the first gives zero with certainty and the second gives one with certainty.

For two independent qubits in pure states, the amplitude of each bit pair is the product of one amplitude from each qubit. Now start with both qubits at zero, apply a Hadamard to the first, and use that qubit as the control of a CNOT targeting the second. The resulting amplitudes are 1/21/\sqrt21/2​ for 00 and 11, and zero for 01 and 10. No two single-qubit amplitude lists can produce this: nonzero products for 00 and 11 would require nonzero products for 01 and 10 as well. This is entanglement. The pure state of the pair cannot be written as separate state vectors for its two parts.

Sequences of H and T gates can approximate any single-qubit unitary operation, up to an unobservable overall phase. CNOT supplies the interaction between qubits. Any unitary operation on several qubits can be decomposed into single-qubit operations and CNOT gates. This makes {H,T,CNOT}\{H,T,\mathrm{CNOT}\}{H,T,CNOT} universal for quantum computation: circuits built from them can approximate any unitary operation to any desired accuracy. More precision generally requires more gates.

A digital circuit represents zero and one by ranges of voltages. Small disturbances within those ranges can be removed as gates regenerate the signal. A qubit must preserve the amplitudes and relative phase needed by the computation. An unwanted phase change can spoil the answer even when the probabilities of immediately measuring zero or one have not changed.

Physical qubits interact with their surroundings. An excited (higher-energy) state can lose energy to the environment and decay. Fluctuating electric or magnetic fields can change the relative phase. Uncontrolled interaction with the environment can carry away information about the qubit and destroy coherence, the phase relationships needed for interference. These processes are called relaxation and dephasing.

Control introduces further opportunities for error. A microwave or laser pulse can change the amplitudes or relative phase by the wrong amount. A signal intended for one qubit can disturb its neighbor. The engineering problem is to suppress unwanted interactions while retaining the deliberate interactions needed to compute. Cooling, shielding, vacuum systems, and precisely controlled fields address different parts of that problem, depending on the hardware.

The encoding of a logical qubit distributes its information so that certain errors can be detected and corrected without learning the encoded amplitudes. It is not a collection of identical backups: the no-cloning theorem rules out copying an arbitrary unknown quantum state.

Error-correction circuits repeatedly measure relationships among the physical qubits, according to the chosen encoding scheme. The checks give the same expected results for all valid logical states, but physical errors can change those results. That lets us detect errors without learning the encoded logical amplitudes. The resulting patterns of check results are called error syndromes. A classical decoder is an algorithm that interprets those patterns to infer likely errors and determine corrections. More physical qubits can provide stronger protection when the physical operations are reliable enough for the chosen encoding and decoder. There is no fixed conversion rate from physical qubits to logical qubits.

Given RSA's public modulus NNN, Shor's factoring algorithm divides the work between quantum and classical processors. The quantum circuit produces a sample carrying information about a period. The classical processor recovers a candidate period, checks it, and calculates the factors. The quantum gates determine the probabilities of the possible samples; they do not test candidate factors.

To turn a period into factors, choose aaa relatively prime to NNN, and consider

f(x)=ax mod N.f(x)=a^x\bmod N.f(x)=axmodN.

The smallest positive rrr for which ar≡1(modN)a^r\equiv1\pmod Nar≡1(modN) is the order of aaa modulo NNN, and it is the period of fff.

The residues relatively prime to NNN form a finite group under multiplication modulo NNN. Their number, φ(N)\varphi(N)φ(N), is Euler's totient. The powers of aaa form a subgroup, a subset that is itself a group under the same operation. This subgroup has rrr elements. Lagrange's theorem says that the size of a subgroup divides the size of the group, so rrr divides φ(N)\varphi(N)φ(N). Since φ(N)<N\varphi(N)<Nφ(N)<N for N>1N>1N>1, we know r<Nr<Nr<N. We will use that bound when interpreting the quantum measurement; we do not need to calculate φ(N)\varphi(N)φ(N) first.

If rrr is even, set z=ar/2 mod Nz=a^{r/2}\bmod Nz=ar/2modN. Then

z2≡1(modN),(z−1)(z+1)≡0(modN).z^2\equiv1\pmod N, \qquad (z-1)(z+1)\equiv0\pmod N.z2≡1(modN),(z−1)(z+1)≡0(modN).

Each prime factor of N=pqN=pqN=pq must divide z−1z-1z−1 or z+1z+1z+1, because NNN divides their product. If both primes divided the same term, zzz would be 111 or −1-1−1 modulo NNN. When neither is true, the factors are split between the two terms. Euclid's algorithm finds them by calculating the greatest common divisors gcd⁡(z−1,N)\gcd(z-1,N)gcd(z−1,N) and gcd⁡(z+1,N)\gcd(z+1,N)gcd(z+1,N). Finding the period is the expensive step on a classical computer.

The input and result registers are now entangled. If they were independent, each would have its own amplitude list. The amplitude for any pair (x, y) would equal the input amplitude for x multiplied by the result amplitude for y.

Our table shows why. The pairs (0, 1) and (1, 2) have nonzero amplitudes, but (0, 2) and (1, 1) have zero amplitudes. The logical qubits must support reliable gates as well as reliable storage. For factoring, modular exponentiation accounts for much of the work. The Fourier transform is a comparatively small part of the circuit.

The Clifford gates are the operations that can be built from HHH, CNOT, and the phase gate SSS, which advances the relative phase by π/2\pi/2π/2. Starting with qubits initialized to ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩, Clifford gates and measurements in the zero/one basis alone give circuits that can be simulated efficiently on a classical computer. They do not provide universal quantum computation.

The T and Toffoli gates are non-Clifford operations: they lie outside that restricted family. A common error-correcting scheme, the surface code, uses local checks on a two-dimensional arrangement of qubits. Computation must be fault-tolerant, meaning that errors in individual components, including the correction machinery, are contained rather than allowed to spread into an uncorrectable logical error.

The T gate must act on the encoded logical amplitudes while preserving the protection of the code. Applying physical T gates independently to the qubits of a surface code does not implement a protected logical T.

In surface-code architectures, supplying non-Clifford operations requires additional machinery. One approach consumes specially prepared auxiliary states called magic states. For example, the state used to supply a T gate is

Outcome zero leaves the data with amplitudes (α,eiπ/4β)(\alpha,e^{i\pi/4}\beta)(α,eiπ/4β), the desired T operation. Outcome one leaves (eiπ/4α,β)(e^{i\pi/4}\alpha,\beta)(eiπ/4α,β). The auxiliary state has been measured and must be replaced for another T gate.

This construction is called gate injection: consuming an auxiliary state to implement a gate. Feed-forward means using a measurement result to choose a subsequent operation, such as the S correction above. Preparing the resource state supplies what the Clifford operations cannot do alone.

A magic-state factory dedicates physical qubits, gates, and time to producing those states reliably. Distillation protocols consume multiple imperfect resource states to obtain fewer, cleaner ones. Other approaches, including magic-state cultivation, change how the states are prepared and checked. A computation that needs these states must wait if the factory has not produced them.

A factory supplies auxiliary quantum states to the computation. Clifford operations, measurements, and classical feedback use them to enact non-Clifford gates. Encoded states throughout this system still require error correction.

These are logical resource estimates. Each protected Toffoli operation can require many physical operations and additional auxiliary states. Turning a gate count into a runtime requires the circuit's dependencies, opportunities for parallelism, factory throughput, and classical response times.

Under its superconducting-hardware assumptions, Google's study estimated fewer than 500,000 physical qubits and a runtime of minutes for this elliptic-curve attack.

For RSA-2048, whose public modulus is 2,048 bits long, Craig Gidney's 2025 estimate put the physical requirement below a million noisy qubits, with a runtime below a week, under specified assumptions about gate errors, connectivity, error-correction cycles, and classical control. These numbers change as algorithms, codes, and hardware improve. There is no single qubit count that defines a CRQC independently of its target and operating conditions.

Error correction remains a substantial part of the machine. An uncorrected physical error probability that looks small for one gate is disastrous over a long computation. As a simple illustration, a billion vulnerable operations, each independently failing with probability 10−610^{-6}10−6, would produce about a thousand failures on average. We need a much smaller logical error rate across the whole computation.

An error-correction scheme has an error threshold: under its assumed noise model, this is the physical error level below which adding redundancy improves protection. Surface-code constructions show how to do this, including the additional machinery needed for a universal set of logical gates.

The syndrome measurements keep the classical decoder busy throughout the calculation. It must infer the likely errors and determine the corrections, often tracking them in a classical record rather than immediately applying a physical correction.

Both the bandwidth and the computation can be enormous. If 100,000 error checks each produce one syndrome bit every microsecond, the raw stream is 101110^{11}1011 bits per second: 12.5 gigabytes per second. An illustrative larger system with ten million such checks would produce 1.25 terabytes per second, before compression or communications overhead. The measurements continue throughout the calculation, potentially for hours or days.

The decoder must compare check results from neighboring qubits over successive rounds to infer likely error patterns. Under their surface-code and decoder assumptions, Camps and colleagues estimated petaflop-scale classical capacity for decoding in a 1,000-logical-qubit chemistry calculation: roughly 101510^{15}1015 floating-point operations per second. Their model converts CPU decoder timings into equivalent floating-point capacity.

Throughput and latency both matter. The classical system must keep pace with incoming measurements, and it must deliver the information needed by later quantum operations. A growing backlog slows the computation. Google's Willow real-time decoding experiment ran error-correction rounds every 1.11.11.1 microseconds, while decoder latency averaged 636363 microseconds. Several rounds can be in flight at once; the sustained processing rate must keep up with new rounds.

Reducing the logical-qubit requirement does not eliminate the physical-qubit overhead, the gate count, or the classical decoding workload. A CRQC is an integrated quantum and classical computer.

Conflating artificial intelligence with quantum computing is nonsense. Today's mainstream AI systems are trained and run on classical computers. Shor's algorithm does not depend on a trained model. There are specific research connections, including neural networks for decoding quantum error syndromes. In that work, classical software interprets the quantum processor's error measurements. It does not make the two technologies interchangeable. A sales pitch that simply bundles “AI” and “quantum” together is asking for your money with two buzzwords.

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) supplies replacements for vulnerable key exchanges and signature schemes, using different mathematical assumptions. It runs on classical computers. NIST's first three finalized standards cover key establishment and digital signatures. Their security relies on mathematical problems that Shor's algorithm does not solve.

For readers interested in the algorithms, I have implemented classical and post-quantum cryptography in pure Rust. The code is available in my cryptography repository.

How soon a system needs to adopt post-quantum cryptography depends partly on how long its information must remain secret. An adversary can retain a vulnerable key exchange and the encrypted traffic associated with it, then attack the exchange when a suitable machine becomes available. NIST calls this harvest now, decrypt later. Upgrading the systems later does not retrieve the copies already recorded.
