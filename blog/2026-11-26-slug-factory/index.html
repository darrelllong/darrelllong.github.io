Slug Factory: Integer Factoring in Rust

November 26, 2026
mathematicsalgorithmsrustnumber-theory

Slug Factory is an integer-factoring program I wrote in Rust. It provides a factor command and a library interface.

$ factor 18446744073709551617
18446744073709551617: 274177 67280421310721

The program combines Pollard rho, a self-initializing multiple-polynomial quadratic sieve, and a general number field sieve. It removes small factors and recognizes prime powers before applying the more expensive methods. Its arithmetic comes from my rump multiprecision library, with random generators and probability functions supplied by entropy. The implementation is in Rust, and the code is available under the BSD 3-Clause license.

The number field sieve implements polynomial selection, relation collection, filtering, sparse linear algebra over the two-element field, and algebraic square roots. It supports distributed computation and saved runs that can be resumed. The matrix solver can use either block Lanczos or block Wiedemann.

Why Factoring Is Interesting

Multiplying two large primes is easy; recovering them from their product can be much harder. RSA depends on this difference. The factoring algorithms are also interesting in their own right, combining probability, number theory, and linear algebra.

Pollard rho searches for collisions modulo an unknown prime factor. The quadratic sieve finds products that are squares by solving equations on prime-exponent parities. The number field sieve extends that method to arithmetic in a number field.

The implementation involves memory-efficient sieving, sparse matrices, and parallel work across cores and machines. Every reported factor is checked by exact division, and the complete factorization is checked against the input.

Pollard Rho

Suppose NNN is composite and ppp is an unknown prime divisor. Choose a starting value and iterate a simple polynomial modulo NNN:

xi+1=xi2+c(modN).x_{i+1}=x_i^2+c\pmod N.xi+1​=xi2​+c(modN).

xi≡xj(modp)⟹p∣(xi−xj).x_i\equiv x_j\pmod p \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p\mid(x_i-x_j).xi​≡xj​(modp)⟹p∣(xi​−xj​).

To detect a repeated value modulo ppp, compute

d=gcd⁡(∣xi−xj∣,N).d=\gcd(|x_i-x_j|,N).d=gcd(∣xi​−xj​∣,N).

When 1<d<N1<d<N1<d<N, we have found a factor. A gcd of one means that this comparison has found nothing; a gcd of NNN means that the comparison has failed to separate the factors.

The expected cost follows from the birthday calculation. For kkk independent draws from ppp values, the probability of avoiding a collision is

∏j=0k−1(1−jp)≈exp⁡(−k(k−1)2p).\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(1-\frac{j}{p}\right) \approx \exp\left(-\frac{k(k-1)}{2p}\right).j=0∏k−1​(1−pj​)≈exp(−2pk(k−1)​).

A collision becomes likely when kkk is of order p\sqrt pp​. Modeling the polynomial as a random map gives rho the same expected cost. For two similarly sized prime factors, ppp is about N\sqrt NN​, giving about N1/4N^{1/4}N1/4 modular steps. Rho is useful for finding relatively small factors of large integers.

Slug Factory uses Brent's version of rho. It compares a moving point against a saved point in stretches whose lengths double. It also batches the gcd computations. For a batch of differences, form

D=∏j(x−yj)(modN)D=\prod_j(x-y_j)\pmod ND=j∏​(x−yj​)(modN)

and take one gcd⁡(D,N)\gcd(D,N)gcd(D,N). Any factor dividing one of those differences divides the product. If the batch gives NNN, the program goes back through the individual differences to try to separate the factors. Batching reduces the number of gcd computations.

The Rho Algorithm

Brent's cycle search uses the following procedure:

  1. Choose the starting value yyy and the polynomial constant ccc. Set the stretch length r=1r=1r=1.
  2. Save x=yx=yx=y, then advance yyy by rrr polynomial evaluations.
  3. Advance through the next rrr values, accumulating x−yx-yx−y into the batch product. At each batch boundary, compute its gcd with NNN.
  4. A proper divisor ends the search. A gcd of NNN triggers a replay of that batch with individual gcds; if the replay fails, start a new polynomial.
  5. If every gcd was one, double rrr and repeat from step 2.

The factoring driver gives rho a bounded amount of work before moving to a sieve. When any method finds a divisor ddd, the driver factors both ddd and N/dN/dN/d until the remaining factors pass its primality test.

Sieving

Rather than trial-divide every Q(x)Q(x)Q(x) by every base prime, solve Q(x)≡0(modp)Q(x)\equiv0\pmod pQ(x)≡0(modp) once for each prime. Each root gives an arithmetic progression of positions divisible by ppp. Walk those progressions and add approximately log⁡p\log plogp to an array. Positions whose accumulated score accounts for most of log⁡∣Q(x)∣\log|Q(x)|log∣Q(x)∣ are worth checking by exact division.

The factor base is chosen accordingly: for an odd prime not dividing NNN, the congruence has roots when NNN is a quadratic residue modulo ppp.

As the interval grows, the values of a single polynomial grow too. Slug Factory changes polynomials to keep the values small, using the multiple-polynomial quadratic sieve. Its polynomials have the form

q(x)=Ax2+2Bx+C,B2−kN=AC,q(x)=Ax^2+2Bx+C, \qquad B^2-kN=AC,q(x)=Ax2+2Bx+C,B2−kN=AC,

where kkk is a small multiplier and AAA is a product of factor-base primes. These polynomials satisfy

(Ax+B)2≡Aq(x)(modN).(Ax+B)^2\equiv A q(x)\pmod N.(Ax+B)2≡Aq(x)(modN).

The exponents contributed by AAA are included in the relation. Choosing the signs of square roots modulo the primes dividing AAA, then combining them by the Chinese remainder theorem, produces a family of suitable values of BBB. Much of the setup can be reused across that family. This is the self-initializing part of the algorithm.

The QS Algorithm

The implementation proceeds as follows:

  1. Choose the multiplier kkk, factor-base bound, and sieve interval. Compute the square roots of kNkNkN modulo each factor-base prime.
  2. Construct a polynomial family by choosing AAA and the corresponding values of BBB and CCC.
  3. For each polynomial, sieve an interval of xxx values using the roots modulo the base primes and their powers.
  4. Trial-divide the promising values to obtain exact relations. Retain partial relations with one allowed large prime; two partials containing the same large prime can be combined, making its exponent even.
  5. Build the parity matrix, filter it, and solve for dependencies. Small matrices use elimination; larger ones use block Lanczos.
  6. Reconstruct XXX and YYY from each dependency and compute the gcds. Collect more relations or try another dependency when necessary.

Polynomial families can be sieved independently by different workers. The matrix stage combines the relations from all of them.

The General Number Field Sieve

GNFS has six stages: polynomial selection, factor-base construction, relation collection, filtering, sparse linear algebra, and square roots. The objective is again a congruence X2≡Y2(modN)X^2\equiv Y^2\pmod NX2≡Y2(modN). The relations now involve both integers and algebraic numbers.

Filtering and Sparse Linear Algebra

Filtering is structured elimination before the main matrix solve. With relations as columns, a row containing only one nonzero forces that relation's coefficient to be zero. Remove it, then repeat because the removal may create further singletons. A row with two nonzeros forces the two relation coefficients to agree, so their columns can be combined by XOR. More general sparse eliminations reduce the matrix while controlling how many nonzeros they introduce. Each combination retains its original relation indices so that a final dependency can be expanded.

The remaining matrix is large and sparse. Ordinary elimination can fill it with nonzeros. Slug Factory instead uses block Lanczos or block Wiedemann for the large systems. Both repeatedly multiply the sparse matrix by blocks of vectors. Sixty-four binary vectors fit into the bits of one machine word, allowing a word XOR to update all 64 together.

Block Lanczos, in Montgomery's formulation, works with the symmetric operator

A=MTM,AV=MT(MV).A=M^{\mathsf T}M, \qquad AV=M^{\mathsf T}(MV).A=MTM,AV=MT(MV).

The program never forms AAA. It applies MMM, then its transpose, using the sparse representation. Starting with a random block, the recurrence builds blocks from the Krylov spaces generated by V,AV,A2V,…V,AV,A^2V,\ldotsV,AV,A2V,…. At each step it forms the small matrix Ti=ViTAViT_i=V_i^{\mathsf T}AV_iTi​=ViT​AVi​ and selects a subspace on which TiT_iTi​ is invertible. The retained blocks are orthogonal under the bilinear form defined by AAA:

WiTAWj=0(i≠j).W_i^{\mathsf T}AW_j=0\qquad(i\ne j).WiT​AWj​=0(i=j).

This lets the recurrence remove components already represented by earlier blocks. The final blocks provide candidate null vectors; a small elimination finds combinations that satisfy Mz=0Mz=0Mz=0.

    This uses a block generalization of the Berlekamp–Massey algorithm.

Running Time

The usual running-time estimates for the two sieves use the notation

LN[α,c]=exp⁡ ⁣((c+o(1)) (log⁡N)α (log⁡log⁡N)1−α).L_N[\alpha,c] =\exp\!\left((c+o(1))\,(\log N)^\alpha \,(\log\log N)^{1-\alpha}\right).LN​[α,c]=exp((c+o(1))(logN)α(loglogN)1−α).

The quadratic sieve has estimated complexity LN[1/2,1]L_N[1/2,1]LN​[1/2,1]. For the general number field sieve it is LN[1/3,(64/9)1/3]L_N[1/3,(64/9)^{1/3}]LN​[1/3,(64/9)1/3]. The smaller exponent makes the number field sieve faster for sufficiently large general integers.

Timings

All of these runs used NVIDIA DGX Sparks, with 20 CPU workers per machine.

Elapsed time for complete factorizations. Top: Hathor and Sekhmet, means of three runs completed November 26. Bottom: four Sparks working together, runs completed November 23. Both use block Lanczos. Timing data.

The single-machine ladder runs from 100 to 115 digits. At 100 digits, the complete calculation took about three and a half minutes on Sekhmet and four minutes on Hathor. At 115 digits, it took about twenty-one and twenty-two minutes. Sekhmet used its local hard disk; Hathor accessed that disk over NFS.

The four-machine ladder reaches 140 digits. The 120-digit input took twelve minutes, and the 140-digit input took about an hour and thirty-eight minutes. Those timings include polynomial selection, sieving, the matrix calculation, and square roots.

Source Code
