![Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. looking puzzled and disappointed toward the University of California, Santa Cruz entrance sign.](/images/king-at-ucsc.png)

*If Dr. King were to visit UCSC*

I spent thirty-five years at the University of California, Santa Cruz. It was always highly politicized. Over the decades, the politics intensified, and the range of views one could express without social or professional consequences grew narrower.

My concern is political monoculture. Scholars should be free to pursue Marxist, conservative, liberal, or other arguments and to challenge one another's conclusions. Political agreement should never be a requirement for hiring, promotion, or a voice in university affairs. The same objection would apply to a university dominated by the Right.

I remember sitting on a committee when Professor John Ellis's name came up as a candidate for service on another committee. Members objected strongly because of what they called “his reprehensible politics.” Ellis's [*Literature Lost*](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300075793/literature-lost/) criticizes the ascendancy of race, gender, and class politics in literary scholarship.

His colleagues wanted to exclude him from ordinary university service because they disliked his politics. I saw the same intolerance in hiring and teaching. It undermines the university's obligation to teach students to think independently.

I examined the faculty's published research. The resulting [spreadsheet](/downloads/ucsc-humanities-research-reviewed.xlsx) documents research descriptions and publications for faculty listed on the Humanities Division and department rosters. The original inventory contains 264 people. Excluding 76 non-Senate appointments leaves **188 Senate faculty: 115 non-emeritus faculty and 73 emeriti**. This main group includes teaching professors and those with the older title of lecturer with security of employment. The excluded group consists of 74 lecturers and two further entries listed under Lecturers and Postdocs. Readers can inspect every classification and source, or use the [CSV version](/downloads/ucsc-humanities-data.csv) to do the arithmetic independently.

Research information was found for **185 of those 188 people**; it was unavailable for three, or 1.6%. Of the 185 with research information, **145 (78.4%) had political research**, and 40 had no political research identified. The definition of political research encompasses government, power, law, war, social hierarchy, collective identity, and economic systems.

The spreadsheet classifies research arguments using research statements, abstracts, publisher descriptions, and available writings. *Left-sympathetic* includes progressive and radical positions. Conventional political history counts as political research. Each judgment has an explanation and sources. A separate column links to faculty research statements or interests where available. The figures count people whose reviewed work includes political research; they do not measure what proportion of each person's publications is political. They also do not measure classroom practice or hiring decisions.

The full Languages and Applied Linguistics roster contains 28 people: 18 non-Senate lecturers excluded from the main analysis and 10 Senate faculty retained. Research information was available for eight of those 10, the denominator in the table. Much of the program's work is practical language instruction. Linguistics is strongly formal, mathematical, and analytical. We used to joke that the linguists belonged in the sciences.

A parent whose child enrolls in literature, history, or philosophy can reasonably expect serious disagreement about the subject's central questions. The department's name does not announce a political commitment. Yet the research reviewed here overwhelmingly favors one side. Parents and students deserve to know that before choosing a program.

UCSC's [introductory American history courses HIS 10A and HIS 10B](https://catalog.ucsc.edu/en/current/general-catalog/courses/his-history) emphasize class, race, ethnicity, and gender in explaining the country's development. The [History of Consciousness catalogue](https://catalog.ucsc.edu/current/general-catalog/courses/hisc-history-of-consciousness) describes HISC 1 as drawing on radical intellectual traditions. HISC 80W, “What Is Imperialism?”, takes the Marxian concept of imperialism as its starting point. HISC 83 introduces critical whiteness studies. A student ought to encounter serious challenges to these approaches and be able to question their premises without apologizing.

In the academic environment I experienced, America's failures were foregrounded while its achievements were left out.

Students studying American history should understand the extraordinary achievements of the founding in the context of the eighteenth century. Judge Douglas Ginsburg's [*A More or Less Perfect Union*](https://pbswisconsin.org/watch/more-or-less-perfect-union-personal-exploration-judge-douglas-ginsburg/a-constitution-in-writing-wk8egm/) shows the framers drawing on earlier governments to design a republic that divided power and protected liberty. They turned those ideas into an agreement among states with competing interests, laying the foundations of enduring self-government. That accomplishment deserves a central place in the classroom.

Our Constitution is the [world's longest-surviving written charter of government](https://www.senate.gov/about/origins-foundations/senate-and-constitution/constitution-day.htm), and our [Declaration of Independence has inspired people around the world to seek freedom and equality](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/declaration). Students should understand what individual liberty, equality before the law, constitutional limits on power, and government by consent have contributed to human freedom.

Reducing that history to a catalogue of departures from twenty-first-century standards obscures the significance of those achievements. An education that leaves students convinced their country is simply terrible and irredeemable substitutes a political verdict for historical understanding.

A dean once explicitly told me that I could not tell my first-generation students, children of immigrants, that in America, if they worked hard, they could achieve anything. My own upbringing and what I had achieved gave me every reason to offer that encouragement. Yet I was forbidden to speak about that opportunity in my teaching.

Critical Race and Ethnic Studies has acted as a political organization in its own name. AMCHA Initiative's [January 2024 report](https://amchainitiative.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/CRES-Report.pdf), especially pp. 5–9, documents these actions through departmental statements, announcements, and conference materials:

- **A political position adopted in the program's name.** CRES's [May 2021 statement on Palestine, archived by AMCHA](https://amchainitiative.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/CRES-Statement-of-Solidarity-with-the-Palestinian-People-on-Nakba-Day.pdf) opposes American support for Israel and calls on other academic units to join collective action. Explaining its stance toward Palestinian dispossession, it declares, “we reject the language of clashes, conflict, and ‘both sides.’”
- **Political agreement as a condition of conference registration.** CRES [co-sponsored the October 2023 inaugural conference](https://criticalzionismstudies.org/battlingihra2023/) of the Institute for the Critical Study of Zionism. Registration initially required agreement with the institute's [political principles](https://criticalzionismstudies.org/points-of-unity/), including opposition to Zionism and support for boycott, divestment, and sanctions. A CRES faculty member involved in organizing it confirms in her [published account](https://manifold.umn.edu/read/ces0901-16/section/f3df687b-d2d6-4e20-a1b9-a9c585510c0b) that the organizers removed the requirement at the executive vice chancellor's request. She explains that the principles remained “a starting point for discussion rather than up for debate.”
- **A departmental strike announcement.** On October 20, 2023, CRES [announced on its news page](https://amchainitiative.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/CRES-News-Screenshot-Save-12.7.23.pdf#page=1) that it was observing a Palestinian-led general strike as a department and would not conduct university business as usual that day.
- **An instruction to skip school and work.** On November 7, CRES [announced departmental participation](https://amchainitiative.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/CRES-News-Screenshot-Save-12.7.23.pdf#page=1) in the November 9 all-day sit-in at the base of campus, telling readers, “Skip school and work.” The [chancellor's subsequent account](https://news.ucsc.edu/2023/11/community-responsiblities/) describes protesters blocking the main entrance for hours, preventing entry and exit, and surrounding drivers who had been waved into the intersection.
- **Organizing direct action with students.** On November 17, CRES promoted the formation of UCSC's Faculty for Justice in Palestine on its departmental website. AMCHA reproduces that announcement and documents CRES faculty among the group's founders. The group's statement included “collaborating with students on direct action” among its commitments. ([Report, pp. 8–9](https://amchainitiative.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/CRES-Report.pdf#page=8).)

On November 13, UCSC's own provost [reminded instructors](https://news.ucsc.edu/2023/11/academic-freedom-and-university-policy/) that university policy prohibits canceling classes to encourage protest attendance and using faculty authority to coerce a student's judgment or conscience.

Faculty may advocate these positions. Their students and colleagues must be equally free to reject them, without finding their own department on the opposing side of a political campaign.

Political research is clearly dominant at every rank. The rank comparison covers the 106 non-emeritus regular professors, excluding the nine teaching professors included in the larger group of 115. Political research was identified for 11 of 16 assistant professors (68.8%), 33 of 38 associates (86.8%), and 38 of 52 full professors (73.1%). Associates have the highest share in this inventory.

My experience on hiring committees left me convinced that political conformity could outweigh scholarly merit. DEI screening made that pressure part of the application process. I believe it helped perpetuate the political monoculture described here.

The Regents' own rule is explicit. Former Standing Order 101.1(d), now [Bylaw 40.3(a)](https://regents.universityofcalifornia.edu/governance/bylaws/bl40.html), states: “No political test shall ever be considered in the appointment and promotion of any faculty member or employee.” The Regents' [comparison of the old and new provisions](https://regents.universityofcalifornia.edu/regmeet/july21/g7attach8.pdf), page 5, confirms that the wording carried over unchanged. The hiring practices I encountered are why I believe this prohibition was ignored.

I sat on hiring committees that hid the political test behind a DEI pledge of allegiance. I was explicitly told that Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.'s principle—that people should be judged by the content of their character—was unacceptable. That is right: even Dr. King was no longer acceptable.

At a training session on DEI statements, I asked, “Suppose an elderly friend of mine with a Nobel Prize wanted to apply, but did not know how to write such a statement. Are you saying you would not even look at his CV?” I was told that they would not. A faculty member then yelled, “I would not even want to work with such a racist, and you are a racist for asking this question!”

I was expected to cower. In modern academia, “racist” is about the worst thing one can call a person. I asked, “Are you calling me a racist?” The faculty member immediately backed down.

In December 2021, [FIRE challenged UCSC's diversity-statement requirement](https://www.fire.org/news/fire-calls-uc-santa-cruz-drop-diversity-statement-mandate-faculty-applicants), documenting how its evaluation rubric penalized applicants who disagreed with the university's positions on diversity, equity, and inclusion. FIRE reported that some searches, including several in engineering and statistics, initially screened applicants on their DEI statements alone. It argued that this procedure excluded dissenters before a full assessment of their academic qualifications and violated freedom of speech and conscience. Calling a political test a diversity statement does not reconcile it with the Regents' prohibition.

On March 19, 2025, the [Regents directed UC to eliminate required diversity statements for new hires](https://www.universityofcalifornia.edu/press-room/statement-uc-board-regents-chair-janet-reilly-use-diversity-statements-university-hiring). That was a necessary correction. The test I describe should never have been imposed.

Removing the form does not end the political screening. It just makes it sub rosa, as are most illegal things in the university.

Santa Cruz owes its students an education in which they can encounter opposing arguments, defend their conclusions, and have their work judged on its merits. Faculty deserve the same freedom. The Regents have forbidden political tests. The university should enforce that rule.

On February 20, 2019, my colleague Ethan Miller, a professor in the Baskin School of Engineering, asked the Academic Senate to adopt the University of Chicago's free-speech principles. His [resolution in the Senate meeting packet](/downloads/ucsc-academic-senate-2019-02-20-call.pdf#page=22), printed pages 19–20, called on the university to protect open debate even when members of its community found the ideas offensive or wrong.

The [Committee on Academic Freedom's written response](/downloads/ucsc-academic-senate-2019-02-20-call.pdf#page=24), printed pages 21–22, argued that existing UC policies made the resolution redundant and that it blurred the distinction between academic freedom and freedom of speech. It also objected to FIRE's politics. Its concluding recommendation was explicit: “Given what appears to be FIRE’s complex political agenda and backing, in our judgment it is not advisable to sign on to a statement that can allow UCSC to appear on a list of FIRE successes on university campuses.”

FIRE is a [nonpartisan civil-liberties organization](https://www.fire.org/about-us/about-us-faqs), and its defense of speech crosses political lines. In 2017, before Miller's proposal, it [defended a Drexel professor facing investigation over controversial tweets](https://www.fire.org/news/drexel-publicly-promised-professor-freedom-expression-privately-pursues-investigation), including criticism of the U.S. military. It later [challenged Florida's Stop WOKE Act](https://www.fire.org/research-learn/fires-news-coverage-and-resources-about-floridas-stop-woke-act) over restrictions on teaching about race and sex, just as it challenged UCSC's DEI screening. FIRE's presumed politics became another cudgel against Miller. His proposal should have been judged on its principles, regardless of who else supported them.

As Miller records in [his blog](https://www.ethanmiller.org/blog/lack-of-freedom-of-speech-at-uc-santa-cruz/), more than 90% of the faculty voting rejected the resolution. He was pilloried for it, and personal attacks soon followed.
