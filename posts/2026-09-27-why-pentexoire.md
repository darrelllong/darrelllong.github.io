I wanted a single-word domain name. That is difficult. Most recognizable words have already been taken, and adding numbers or inventing a spelling rather defeats the purpose. I found mine in the Middle Ages.

*The Travels of Sir John Mandeville* is a splendid read, and Umberto Eco's *Baudolino* is my favorite book. Both take us into the land of Prester John, the Christian priest and king whose kingdom lay somewhere beyond Persia in the medieval imagination. Pentexoire is one part of those extensive dominions. At the end of chapter XXIX, Mandeville introduces the name:

> And men clepe his realm the isle of Pentexoire.

*Clepe* means *call*. But the next chapter opens by describing a much larger domain:

> This emperor, Prester John, holds full great land, and hath many full noble cities and good towns in his realm, and many great diverse isles and large.

In this [Cotton manuscript version, in modern spelling](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/782/782-h/782-h.htm#page178), Mandeville explains that rivers divide the land into what he calls islands, and that Prester John also possesses islands in the sea. Nyse is the chief city of Pentexoire; later in the chapter, Mistorak is explicitly another island beside it, also under Prester John's rule. Pentexoire belongs to his domain, but does not encompass it. Mandeville gives him seventy-two provinces, each with its own king.

Mandeville's book belongs to the fourteenth century, but [the legend of Prester John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prester_John) was already some two centuries old. In 1145, Otto of Freising recorded a story told by Hugh, bishop of Jabala in Syria. Far beyond Persia and Armenia, Hugh said, a Christian ruler named John had defeated the Persian kings. He was both priest and king, descended from the Magi, and had intended to bring his army to the aid of Jerusalem. Unfortunately, he had been unable to cross the Tigris. [Otto's account](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/otto-prester.asp) already holds the promise of a powerful Christian ally beyond the lands of the crusaders' enemies.

Around 1165, a letter began circulating in Europe purporting to come from this ruler. It was addressed to the Byzantine emperor Manuel I Komnenos. The *Letter of Prester John* was a forgery. Its author is unknown; its Latin form and political concerns point to a Western European setting, often associated with the German lands. The precise place of composition and the author's purpose remain disputed.

The [letter itself](https://blackcentraleurope.com/sources/1000-1500/a-letter-from-prester-john-ca-1165-1170/) is splendidly immodest. Seventy-two kings pay tribute to its author. His land possesses extraordinary wealth, fabulous animals, and a river that carries stones instead of water. He promises to lead an immense army to the Holy Sepulchre. He also manages to lecture Manuel on his mortality and question his religious orthodoxy. Whoever wrote it had more in mind than entertaining readers with exotic geography; the supposed eastern monarch could be used to belittle the very real emperor in Constantinople.

The wonders help explain the letter's hold on the imagination. Here was a Christian kingdom with fabulous creatures, inexhaustible riches, and an enormous army. The letter also promised a society without poverty or robbery. A king could imagine an ally; a priest could imagine a vast Christian people beyond the familiar world; someone hearing the story could imagine a land of abundance where life was better. Its marvels made that promise vivid. Beginning in the twelfth century, the story spread through courts and religious houses, and [vernacular versions and romances carried it to a wider lay audience](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.18163682), who could hear it told as well as read it.

Even a pope took the distant ruler seriously. On September 27, 1177, [Alexander III addressed a letter to John, king of the Indians](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Prester_John), and entrusted his physician Philip with a mission to him. Alexander hoped to bring this eastern Christian monarch into closer communion with Rome. We have the pope's letter, although no record of Philip reaching his destination. Prester John had become someone to whom the pope could send an envoy.

Whatever the author's intention, I can imagine how comforting the central proposition must have been to a hard-pressed Christian ruler in the twelfth century. Beyond the lands of his enemies stood another Christian king, richer and more powerful than any ruler he knew, with an army that could attack those enemies from the other side.

Islamic expansion and invasion were concrete military realities. The Seljuk victory at [Manzikert in 1071](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Manzikert) helped open Byzantine Anatolia to Turkish conquest and settlement. In 1144, Zengi captured Edessa, destroying the first of the crusader states to fall. The expedition launched in response, the [Second Crusade](https://www.worldhistory.org/Second_Crusade/), failed. The prospect of a powerful ally to the east must have been welcome after those defeats.

The threats also came from other directions. Pecheneg and Cuman horsemen had attacked the Byzantine frontier from the eastern steppe. The [Pechenegs were defeated again in 1122](https://www.encyclopediaofukraine.com/display.asp?linkpath=pages%5CP%5CE%5CPechenegs.htm), within the lifetime of men who first heard the Prester John story. These were among the peoples whom the settled empires described as barbarians. An eastern frontier could bring a new invading army as readily as a merchant caravan.

On Europe's northern and eastern frontiers, Christian rulers also fought pagan peoples. In the [Wendish Crusade of 1147](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendish_Crusade), Saxon and Danish forces attacked the pagan Slavs along the Baltic, while other crusaders marched toward the Holy Land.

Nor did Christian neighbors guarantee security. Manuel faced Norman attacks from Sicily as well as rivalry with the German emperor Frederick Barbarossa. His wars and diplomacy also involved Hungary, Serbia, and the crusader principalities. [The record of his reign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_I_Komnenos) makes it clear how many directions an emperor had to watch.

Prester John offered relief from that geography. The hostile power on your frontier would have to worry about its own frontier. Somewhere beyond it was a king who could afford to help, whose soldiers were numerous, and who shared your faith. I can understand the appeal of that hope, especially when the news from the battlefield was bad.

Mandeville gives us plenty to see in those dominions. In [chapter XXX](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/782/782-h/782-h.htm#page178), there is a Gravelly Sea, whose sand and gravel rise and fall in waves without any water. Fish live in it nonetheless. A river carries precious stones and great rocks into that sea, also without water. It runs three days each week, when no one dares enter; on the other days it can be crossed. Nearby, trees emerge from the ground at sunrise, grow and bear fruit before noon, then shrink back into the earth by sunset. His palace at Susa has crystal windows, tables of emerald and amethyst, and gems that light the rooms at night.

Then there are the creatures. Mandeville describes horned wild men, man-eating giants, white elephants, unicorns, and dragons. On Taprobane, an island whose king owes allegiance to Prester John, [ants as large as dogs guard hills of gold](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/782/782-h/782-h.htm#page198). Getting the gold away from them requires some ingenuity.

Eco brings the medieval bestiary to life in *Baudolino*. On the journey toward Prester John's kingdom, the travelers reach Pndapetzim, the city of Deacon John, the priest-king's designated successor. Gavagai is a skiapod, moving on one leg with an enormous foot that also serves as a sunshade. There are [headless blemmyae with faces in their torsos, and panotians who wrap themselves in their enormous ears](https://www.independent.co.uk/arts-entertainment/books/reviews/baudolino-by-umberto-eco-trans-william-weaver-139665.html), as well as giants and pygmies.

The inhabitants have plenty to argue about. Their [different Christian doctrines](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0096144220910139) matter far more to them than their different bodies: the skiapods are Arians, the blemmyae Adoptionists, and the giants Artotyrites, who use bread and cheese in their Eucharist. Eco gives these people conversation, convictions, and a city to share. The illustration above gathers some of the wonders of these two books, with the river of stones in the distance.

Pentexoire gave me the single-word domain name I wanted, and a connection to two books I love.
