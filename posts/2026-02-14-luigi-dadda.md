![Luigi Dadda](/posts/images/Luigi-Dadda.jpg)

Luigi Dadda spent more than half a century at the Politecnico di Milano, working in computer arithmetic, building its computing facilities, and educating a generation of engineers. His student [Domenico Ferrari](/blog/2026-02-14-domenico-ferrari) was the doctoral advisor of my own advisor, [Jehan-François Pâris](/blog/2026-02-14-jehan-francois-paris).

Dadda was born into a farming family in Lodi on April 29, 1923. During the Second World War, he bicycled fifty kilometers to retrieve textbooks after a science bookstore in Milan was bombed. He studied electrical engineering at the Politecnico and completed his *laurea* in 1947, with a thesis on signal transmission over a microwave radio link between Turin and Trieste. He remained as an assistant professor under the mentorship of [Ercole Bottani](/blog/2026-02-06-ercole-bottani).

His early research concerned analog computing. Working with Bottani at the National Research Council, he used electrical networks to model physical systems, including large power networks and magnetic fields described by Laplace's equations.

In 1953, Dadda declined a National Science Foundation grant to study at Caltech in order to take part in the Politecnico's digital-computing project. Marshall Plan funding supported the acquisition of a CRC 102A, and he traveled to San Diego to work with the Computer Research Corporation on its completion. In 1954, he accompanied the machine on its voyage to Italy, with its vacuum tubes carefully packed for the crossing. Installed in Milan, it became Italy's first working digital computer.

Dadda trained students and faculty to use the CRC 102A, organized Italy's first university courses in electronic data processing, and directed the Centro di Calcolo, which served academic and industrial users. He also designed a floating-point arithmetic unit for the machine, effectively doubling its computing power. By the late 1950s, he had established a degree program in electronic engineering at the Politecnico.

His best-known contribution to computer arithmetic came in 1965. The Dadda multiplier uses a carefully arranged tree of partial-sum adders to reduce the stages required for parallel binary multiplication. It remains an influential design for hardware multipliers. In 2016, the IEEE recognized it as a Milestone in electrical engineering, placing it among Italian achievements associated with Alessandro Volta, Guglielmo Marconi, Antonio Pacinotti, and Enrico Fermi.

His later research included architectures for fast convolution, specialized arithmetic units for CERN's Large Hadron Collider, cryptographic applications, and decimal arithmetic. He studied Petri nets for control-system design, while the Politecnico's computer laboratories expanded into programming languages, databases, and networks.

Dadda became a full professor in 1960 and was appointed to the Chair of Electrical Engineering in 1962. He served as Rector of the Politecnico from 1972 to 1984. His work in professional organizations began earlier: he co-founded the Associazione Italiana per l'Informatica e il Calcolo Automatico in 1961 and served as its president from 1967 to 1970. He also co-founded and edited *Rivista di Informatica*.

Beyond Italy, Dadda promoted the European Informatics Network, developed in the late 1970s as COST 11 under the European Economic Community to link computing centers across Europe. From 1980 to 1982, he chaired the Italian Prime Minister's Science and Technology Committee. He also served on a technical committee and advised the board of CSELT, Telecom Italia's research and development organization.

After retiring from the Politecnico in 1998, he helped establish the Università della Svizzera Italiana in Lugano. From 2000 until 2012, he was president of its Advanced Learning and Research Institute, whose work focused on embedded systems.

Dadda was an IEEE Fellow and later a Life Fellow for his contributions to computer arithmetic and digital signal processing. In 1976, he was named Cavaliere di Gran Croce dell'Ordine al Merito della Repubblica Italiana.

His visit to San Diego was the first of several California connections in this academic line. Ferrari spent twenty-five years at Berkeley; Pâris taught at UC San Diego, where I studied with him; and I went on to UC Santa Cruz. The connection between the Politecnico and the University of California extends across four generations.

Luigi Dadda died in Milan on October 26, 2012, at eighty-nine.
