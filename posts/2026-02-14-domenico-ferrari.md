![Domenico Ferrari](/posts/images/Domenico-Ferrari.jpg)

Domenico Ferrari is my academic grandfather. I did not study with him directly, but his work and teaching reached me through my doctoral advisor, [Jehan-François Pâris](/blog/2026-02-14-jehan-francois-paris). His career joined computer systems performance, the development of Berkeley Unix, and research on quality of service in computer networks.

Ferrari came from Piacenza. He earned his *Dottore in Ingegneria Elettronica* at the Politecnico di Milano in 1963 under [Luigi Dadda](/blog/2026-02-14-luigi-dadda), whose mentor was [Ercole Bottani](/blog/2026-02-06-ercole-bottani). After a brief appointment as an assistant professor, he moved to the United States. He joined Berkeley's Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Sciences in 1970 and remained there for twenty-five years.

At Berkeley, Ferrari became a full professor and chaired the Computer Science Division from 1983 to 1987. He returned to the Politecnico as a visiting professor in 1976–77 and retired from Berkeley as Professor Emeritus in 1995.

His early research applied queueing theory and workload characterization to the measurement and modeling of time-sharing systems and virtual memory. His 1978 book, *Computer Systems Performance Evaluation*, became a standard reference. In a 1984 technical report, he argued that performance evaluation had become too isolated from the rest of computing and should be incorporated into the design of computer architectures and software. He received the A.A. Michelson Award for contributions to computer metrics in 1987, the year he was also named an IEEE Fellow.

When Bob Fabry stepped aside from Berkeley's Computer Systems Research Group in the mid-1980s, Ferrari and Susan Graham assumed responsibility for its administration and for Berkeley Unix. During their tenure, the group released 4.3BSD, refining the TCP/IP implementation and the Berkeley Fast File System introduced in 4.2BSD. BSD provided a common platform for research in networking and distributed systems. Its code contributed to FreeBSD, OpenBSD, NeXTSTEP, and eventually macOS; its socket interface remains familiar to generations of programmers.

In 1989, Ferrari founded the Tenet Group at Berkeley and the International Computer Science Institute. The group investigated how packet-switched networks could provide the timing guarantees needed for real-time audio and video. Its Tenet I and II protocol suites combined scheduling, admission control, and resource reservation to support those guarantees across wide-area networks.

The group's research addressed delay jitter, traffic specification, rate-controlled scheduling, and real-time transport. These ideas contributed to later work on Integrated Services and RSVP. Ferrari directed Tenet until his retirement in 1995.

Ferrari supervised more than three dozen Berkeley Ph.D. students between the early 1970s and 2000. They went on to faculty positions at Bologna, Carnegie Mellon, Cornell, Rice, UC San Diego, Waterloo, and Berkeley, as well as industrial research laboratories. Pâris was among them.

After retiring, Ferrari returned to Piacenza and founded the Center for Research on the Applications of Telematics to Organizations and Society at the Università Cattolica del Sacro Cuore. CRATOS applied networking research to telemedicine, government services, and the developing internet economy. In 2000, he was appointed *Professore Ordinario per Chiara Fama* in computer science and established a master's program in Management in the Network Economy.

Ferrari became an ACM Fellow in 2001. In 2006, he was the first Italian to receive the ACM SIGCOMM Award. The citation recognized his contributions to Internet quality-of-service architecture, his leadership at ICSI, his management of the Berkeley Unix project, and his mentorship of doctoral students.
